An acquisition in Bolivia follows, in its general structure, the same logic as in any other jurisdiction: due diligence, negotiation of terms, signing, and closing. What changes are the specific details of each stage (what to look for in due diligence, which transaction structure makes more sense, and what can slow the process down between signing and closing) and those details are what determine whether a transaction moves smoothly or stalls at the wrong stage.

## What Due Diligence Covers

Due diligence in a Bolivian acquisition typically examines, alongside the usual financial and commercial matters, a set of topics that deserve particular attention in this market:

- The target company's corporate standing: whether its filings, minutes and bylaw amendments are up to date and in order
- Tax compliance, including contingencies that may not yet be reflected in a formal audit process
- Sector licences and authorisations, and whether they transfer to the new controller or require an independent process
- Employment contracts and accrued labour liabilities, an item that in Bolivia often carries more risk than foreign buyers initially anticipate
- Title to key assets, particularly real estate, where title has not been recently audited

None of these items is unique to Bolivia, but the relative weight each one carries in the transaction's risk profile can differ from what a foreign buyer is used to assessing in other markets.

## Structuring the Deal: Assets or Shares

An early decision in any acquisition is whether to structure the transaction as an asset purchase or a share purchase. Each structure shifts risk differently: an asset purchase lets the buyer choose specifically which assets and liabilities it acquires, while a share purchase transfers the entire company, with its history and contingencies, unless specific protective mechanisms are negotiated.

The choice is not only a matter of risk appetite; it also carries tax and operational implications (contracts that may require the counterparty's consent to transfer, licences that cannot be transferred outside the holding legal entity) that are worth assessing before the structure is locked into the term sheet.

> Deal structure is not a technical detail resolved after the price is agreed; it often defines the price.

## From Signing to Closing

Between signing and the actual closing of the transaction, there is usually a period during which the agreed conditions precedent are satisfied: regulatory approvals where the sector requires them, third-party consents to transfer relevant contracts, and, in some cases, competition clearances. How long this period lasts depends directly on how many conditions precedent exist and how much control the parties have over satisfying them.

A frequent mistake is underestimating how long approvals that depend on third parties (particularly regulatory bodies) take, and setting an optimistic closing date in the contract that later has to be renegotiated.

## After Closing

Post-closing integration (aligning systems, organisational culture, and at times renegotiating key commercial relationships) largely determines whether the transaction ends up generating the value projected when the acquisition was decided. The legal aspects of this stage tend to be less visible than those of due diligence or closing, but the obligations assumed in the purchase agreement (representations and warranties, indemnities, transition periods) remain relevant long after the transaction is considered closed.
