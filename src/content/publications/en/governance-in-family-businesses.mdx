There is a common assumption that family businesses need less formal governance than other companies, because trust among the owners replaces the need for written rules. Experience suggests the opposite: precisely because family relationships and shareholder relationships overlap, family businesses need clearer rules, not fewer — and they need to define them before a disagreement makes it urgent.

## The Shareholder Agreement as a Foundational Document

A well-drafted shareholder agreement is not a document signed to satisfy a formality; it is the instrument that anticipates, while the relationship among the owners is still good, how decisions will be resolved once they stop agreeing. Among the points a family business's shareholder agreement should cover with particular care are the majorities required for important decisions, how a shareholder who wants to sell their stake is treated, and what happens if a shareholder dies or loses capacity.

These topics are negotiated far more easily before a concrete conflict exists. Once two branches of the family disagree about the direction of the business, drafting the decision-making rules for the first time becomes part of the conflict itself, rather than the tool for resolving it.

## Separating Ownership From Management

One of the most frequent sources of friction in family businesses is confusing being an owner with having the right to run the company. Not every shareholder necessarily has the same ability or the same interest in managing the business day to day, and treating both roles as equivalent generates resentment in both directions — toward the shareholder who manages without being the best qualified, and toward the one who feels excluded from operations despite being an owner.

Formally separating these roles — through a board with clear participation criteria, and an explicit definition of which decisions belong to ownership and which to management — does not eliminate the tension, but it gives it an institutional channel instead of leaving it as a permanent source of informal conflict.

## Planning Succession Before It Becomes Urgent

Succession in a family business rarely fails for lack of a business plan; it fails for lack of an ownership plan. Who inherits the shares, whether those shares come with a role in management, and how heirs who are not actively involved in the business are treated are questions that, without an answer prepared in advance, end up being resolved at the worst possible moment: immediately after the founding shareholder's death or incapacity.

> Well-planned succession is not an event; it is a series of decisions made with time to spare, while there is still room to adjust them.

## When Family Conflict and Shareholder Conflict Overlap

A divorce, a dispute among siblings over an inheritance, or simply the estrangement of two branches of an extended family can, without anyone intending it, turn into a shareholder dispute with consequences for the business. A well-designed shareholder agreement cannot prevent those family conflicts from happening, but it can prevent them from paralysing the company: deadlock-resolution mechanisms, valuation and forced-sale clauses between shareholders, and clear rules on board representation are tools that exist precisely for those scenarios.

No business family plans for conflict, but the ones that survive into a second or third generation are, almost without exception, the ones that prepared for it.
