When two parties from different jurisdictions negotiate a contract with a Bolivian counterparty, the dispute-resolution clause is usually drafted last, almost as a formality. That is a mistake: if the contract ever breaks down, that clause determines how much time, cost and uncertainty the parties will face in enforcing their rights. Arbitration has become, over the past few decades, the preferred route for this kind of contract — but it is not automatically the right choice in every case.

## When Arbitration Makes Sense

Arbitration offers clear advantages in international contracts: a tribunal neutral to both parties, greater confidentiality than court proceedings, and — in most cases — an award that is easier to enforce in other jurisdictions than a foreign court judgment, thanks to the international framework for recognising arbitral awards. These advantages matter more the greater the distance — jurisdictional and in terms of institutional trust — between the parties.

Arbitration is not always the right choice, though. For lower-value contracts, the cost of arbitral proceedings can exceed the amount in dispute. For matters requiring urgent interim relief, ordinary courts sometimes act faster than a tribunal can be constituted. And if both parties already trust the courts of one of the jurisdictions involved, litigating may be simpler and cheaper than arbitrating.

## The Three Decisions in Drafting the Clause

A well-drafted arbitration clause resolves three questions precisely: where, under what rules, and who administers the process.

- **The seat of arbitration**, which determines the procedural law governing the arbitration and which courts have supporting or annulment jurisdiction over the award. The seat does not need to match where the contract is performed.
- **The applicable rules**, which can be those of an arbitral institution — with its own procedural rules — or an ad hoc set such as the UNCITRAL rules, which require the parties to define more details themselves.
- **The administering institution**, if institutional arbitration is chosen, which handles logistical matters such as appointing arbitrators if the parties cannot agree.

## Common Drafting Mistakes

Poorly drafted arbitration clauses — known in practice as pathological clauses — generate disputes about the dispute itself, before the parties ever reach the merits. The most common mistakes include:

| Common mistake | Typical consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Referring imprecisely to a non-existent or ambiguous institution | A preliminary fight over which institution administers the case |
| Not specifying the language of the arbitration | Disputes over what language submissions and hearings are conducted in |
| Combining arbitration and litigation for the same type of dispute | Ambiguity over which route prevails |
| Omitting the number of arbitrators | Default rules apply that neither party had considered |

> An arbitration clause is not drafted for the day the contract is signed — it is drafted for the day, years later, when the contract breaks down and someone reads it closely for the first time.

## Enforcement Considerations

Agreeing to arbitration is not useful if the resulting award cannot be enforced where the counterparty holds assets. Before finalising the clause, it is worth confirming that the chosen seat and the applicable recognition framework allow enforcement of an eventual award both in Bolivia and in whichever jurisdiction the Bolivian — or foreign — counterparty holds its assets. That question, more than any institutional preference, should guide the final decision.

Arbitration is a powerful tool when used for the right reasons and drafted with care. When it is agreed to out of habit, without thinking through the specific case, it can end up being slower and more expensive than the alternative it was meant to avoid.
